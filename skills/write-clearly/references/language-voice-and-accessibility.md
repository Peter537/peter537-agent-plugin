# Language, voice, and accessibility

Use this reference to improve comprehension without erasing the writer's language or identity.

## Work in the source language

- Preserve the current language unless translation is separately requested.
- Infer spelling and locale from repository guidance and consistent surrounding prose; ask only when ambiguity materially affects the result.
- Keep product terms, identifiers, and established loanwords stable.
- For mixed-language material, identify which spans are intentionally untranslated before editing.
- When fluency is insufficient to validate a safe rewrite, limit the result to high-confidence corrections or an audit and report the limitation.

Translation is a distinct task. If the user requests both translation and editing, preserve the translation boundary and do not claim that this skill certifies translation quality.

## Preserve dialect and non-native voice

- Do not “correct” a regional, social, or non-standard variety solely because it differs from standardized professional language.
- Separate genuine ambiguity from unfamiliar but valid language.
- Do not infer or imitate dialect from nationality, ethnicity, location, or another demographic label.
- Preserve the author's level of formality, directness, idiom, and emotional openness unless it conflicts with the document's function or explicit house style.
- Do not add idioms, slang, complexity, or decorative variation to make non-native writing appear more fluent.

## Calibrate author voice

Use samples only when they are user-provided, repository-authoritative, or clearly attributed to the intended author. Prefer several samples from the same language, genre, audience, and period.

Observe:

- sentence and paragraph shape;
- contractions and person;
- punctuation and asides;
- directness and hedging;
- preferred terminology;
- examples, analogy, humor, and emotional range;
- headings, lists, openings, and endings;
- deliberate fragments, repetition, or formality.

Distinguish a repeatable voice feature from one accidental phrase. Exact human edits and explicit preferences outweigh statistical imitation. Never expose private samples or quote more of them than the task requires.

## Write for global readers

Prefer clear and unambiguous wording, stable terminology, explicit antecedents, and standard structures when they preserve meaning. Put conditions near the action or consequence they govern. Define unfamiliar abbreviations and avoid unexplained culture-specific references, slang, or idioms when the audience is global or translation is expected.

Do not remove necessary technical distinctions merely to use shorter words. Repeat a noun when repetition prevents an ambiguous pronoun or mistranslation.

## Improve cognitive accessibility proportionately

Use understandable words, coherent short blocks, clear headings, direct relationships, and manageable sentence structures when appropriate for the audience. Explain unfamiliar terms rather than replacing necessary domain language with an inaccurate simplification.

These practices can improve accessibility, but an editorial review does not certify WCAG conformance, comprehension, localization readiness, or usability. Report such claims only when separately verified by the relevant workflow.
